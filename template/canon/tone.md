# Tone & Style Guide

The reference point is **grounded civic realism** — *The Wire*, long-form local journalism, Robert Caro on Robert Moses. Not soap opera. Not satire. Not utopia.

## What that means

- **People act from interests, not virtues or villainy.** The developer pushing for the riverfront tower isn't evil — he has investors waiting, a quarterly clock, and a real belief that density is good for the city. The neighbors fighting him aren't NIMBYs in caricature — they remember the last "transformative project" that cost them parking, sunlight, and a beloved diner.
- **Every win has a loser, named.** When the city zones a new suburb, somebody owned that land before and somebody else wanted it for something else. Both have names.
- **Specifics beat abstractions.** "A coalition of business interests" is lazy. "Four restaurant owners on Bleeker, a hotel GM, and the chamber's outgoing chair" is the story.
- **The city has institutional memory.** Old fights echo. A councilor's vote on rezoning in 2018 still costs them in 2026. The neighborhood association that beat the highway extension in 1974 is still the neighborhood association.
- **Mixed records.** Even the heroes of one storyline are the obstacle in another. Beloved coach has tax problems. Reformist mayor takes the wrong meeting. This is what "grounded" means.

## What to avoid

- Cackling villains, saints, or characters who only exist to lose.
- Joke names, joke companies, anachronisms.
- Magic, fate, prophecy, "the city itself wanted this."
- Resolution that's too clean. Most things stay messy.
- Generic worldbuilding ("the docks are run by a mysterious crime family"). If something is true, name names and explain how it got that way.

## Length

- Character files: 200–500 words. Enough to know what they want and how they got here.
- Event entries: 100–300 words. What happened, who pushed it, what it cost, what's next.
- Stories / vignettes: 400–1500 words. A news article, a council meeting transcript, a developer's all-hands email, a kid's letter to the editor. Form follows what the moment wants.
- Session notes: as long as needed. Bullets are fine.

## Voice

Third-person, present or past depending on form. Journalistic when the piece is "news." Intimate and specific when the piece is "character." Never breaks the fourth wall — the story doesn't know the player exists.
